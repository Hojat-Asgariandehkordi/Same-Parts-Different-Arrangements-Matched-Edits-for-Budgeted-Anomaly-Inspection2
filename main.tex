\begin{abstract}
An anomaly detector that raises an alarm on a faulty product is useful only if it also tells the inspector where to look. We show that this second step can fail even when detection is perfect. We study a tray that must hold one pushpin per compartment and edit real photographs so that the same pin moves either within its own compartment, which is allowed, or into a neighbouring compartment, which breaks the rule. Because both edits change exactly the same pixels, they reveal precisely which two compartments need checking. A recent detector flags every faulty tray, yet to find nearly all the misplaced pins it also sends the inspector to 11 of the 13 untouched compartments. The cause lies in how the detector is trained: it learns to mark every pin as anomalous whenever one pin is added. Teaching it to mark only the pin that was actually moved reduces the extra checks to less than half a compartment, while every fault is still detected. The effect carries over to real defective trays: retrained from scratch in this way, the detector finds 76--80\% of the faulty compartments while opening fewer than 0.4 intact compartments per tray, whereas the best way of reading the original detector finds 49\% and opens 1.1. Where parts are interchangeable, as in a bag of screws, marking every part remains the better choice. Matched valid and invalid edits thus turn anomaly detection benchmarks into a measure of the work an alarm creates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Visual anomaly detection is usually assessed by whether a system flags defective products~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec,roth2022patchcore,batzner2024}, yet on a production line an alarm is only the first step: an operator must then locate the defect. For \emph{logical} anomalies~\cite{bergmann2022} this second step is particularly demanding. A pushpin tray with one empty and one doubled compartment contains the correct number of intact pins, and only their arrangement is wrong; a detector is helpful if its map points to the two affected compartments, and of little use if it highlights the other thirteen as well. Current benchmarks do not measure this cost. MVTec LOCO~\cite{bergmann2022} reports image-level AUROC and saturated per-region overlap, and neither states how many locations an inspector must examine to recover nearly every problem. Measuring it requires knowing exactly which locations are wrong, which natural logical defects rarely provide; an extra screw in a bag may be any of several identical screws~\cite{fucka2025}.

We obtain exact targets by intervention (\cref{fig:teaser}a). From each held-out normal photograph we move an intact pin either within its compartment (a \emph{valid sham}) or by the same distance into an occupied neighbour (a \emph{fault}). Both edits share the cutout, fill and displacement, so any difference in the detector's response is caused by the rule violation, and the fault fixes the two compartments that must be inspected. A scene alarm then decides whether a tray is opened, and a compartment cutoff fixed on development data decides which compartments are queued; we report the fraction of target compartments found (recall) and the number of untouched compartments queued (cost).

A toy example makes the failure concrete (\cref{fig:teaser}b). Suppose a detector assigns each compartment a score, and the inspector opens every compartment whose score exceeds a cutoff chosen so that 95\% of misplaced pins are found. If the detector was trained to call every pin anomalous once a pin is inserted, the untouched compartments score almost as high as the two affected ones, the cutoff must fall below them, and the inspector opens 13 of 15 compartments to find 2. If it was trained to mark only the inserted pin, the same cutoff separates the two affected compartments from the rest, and the inspector opens 2. Both detectors raise the same alarm and would receive the same image-level AUROC; only the second is useful.

We apply this protocol to SALAD~\cite{fucka2025}, a recent logical anomaly detector whose composition branch scores predicted part maps, and ask two questions: how much inspection effort does a detector that catches every fault still impose, and what in its training determines that effort? Our paper offers the following contributions. As an \textbf{evaluation design}, we introduce matched valid and invalid edits with identical pixel changes, together with a recall--cost criterion calibrated on development data (\cref{sec:method}). As a \textbf{diagnosis}, we show that the released composition branch detects all 138 faults and ranks each above its sham, yet queues 11.07 of 13 untouched compartments per fault at 95\% target recall (\cref{sec:released}). As a \textbf{remedy}, we show that the training target governs this cost: marking only the pasted part reduces it to 0.20--0.45 compartments across two seeds, with paired intervals far from zero, and an optimality argument explains why (\cref{sec:targets,sec:footprint}). Finally, as \textbf{confirmation}, retraining SALAD from scratch with footprint targets more than doubles localization of real pushpin defects and, at operating points fixed in advance, finds more defective compartments while opening fewer intact ones than any policy on the released map; the class-wide target remains preferable for interchangeable parts, and valid shams reverse what fault-only evaluation suggests about appearance detectors (\cref{sec:scratch,sec:native,sec:ambiguous,sec:shams}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
Industrial anomaly detection was first benchmarked on local structural defects such as scratches and dents~\cite{bergmann2019mvtec}, where patch-feature memory banks~\cite{roth2022patchcore} and student--teacher networks~\cite{batzner2024} perform strongly. MVTec LOCO~\cite{bergmann2022} adds logical defects that violate constraints on the number, position or combination of parts and evaluates localization with the saturated per-region overlap (sPRO), which tolerates ambiguous ground truth. Component-based methods segment parts and compare attributes, correspondences or executable rules~\cite{liu2023,peng2025,zhang2026logico,ji2026}, and SALAD~\cite{fucka2025} combines a composition branch on part maps with local and global appearance branches. Many of these detectors train a discriminative head on synthetic defects: CutPaste~\cite{li2021cutpaste} pastes image patches, DRAEM~\cite{zavrtanik2021draem} blends Perlin-masked textures and segments the altered region, LogicAL~\cite{zhao2024} synthesizes logical defects, and SALAD inserts and relabels parts in composition maps. This line of work establishes strong detection and region overlap, but it neither measures how many locations an inspector must open once an alarm fires nor examines how the target mask of the synthetic edits shapes that number. We hold the detector and its edits fixed and study exactly these two questions.

Our evaluation borrows from matched and counterfactual testing outside anomaly detection. Contrast sets~\cite{gardner2020contrast} and counterfactually augmented data~\cite{kaushik2020learning} probe language models with minimally different inputs that carry different labels, and Winoground~\cite{thrush2022} pairs captions that share words but differ in their relations; such pairs reveal shortcut features that ordinary test sets reward~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}. Our valid shams play this role for anomaly maps, since they alter as many pixels as the fault while respecting the rule. Operating points follow split-conformal calibration on normal photographs~\cite{bates2023,angelopoulos2023gentle}, which bounds the false-alarm rate.

\subsection{Problem setting}
\label{sec:setting}
A photograph $x_i$ shows a tray of $|\E|=15$ compartments arranged in a $5{\times}3$ grid, and the published rule requires exactly one pin per compartment~\cite{bergmann2022}. A part extractor maps the photograph to a six-class part map $m_i\in\{0,\dots,5\}^{256\times256}$, and the composition branch $f$ of the detector returns a score map $S_i=f(m_i)\in[0,1]^{256\times256}$ in which higher values indicate anomalies (\cref{fig:pipeline}). For a compartment $e\in\E$, $S_i|_e$ denotes the scores of its pixels; compartment boundaries are fixed once from the released label maps.

\subsection{Matched edits}
\label{sec:edits}
We use all 138 held-out normal pushpin photographs. Using the released class mask and a two-pixel RGB halo, we cut out one intact pin and translate it without resampling, which yields four variants per photograph (\cref{fig:teaser}a, \cref{fig:photos}). The \emph{source} is the unedited photograph. In the \emph{valid sham}, the pin moves within its own compartment, so every compartment still holds one pin. In the \emph{fault}, the pin moves by the same squared displacement into a face-adjacent occupied compartment, leaving its source compartment $a_i$ empty and the recipient $b_i$ with two pins. The \emph{vacancy-only} variant removes the pin without pasting it elsewhere. The sham and the fault share the cutout, the fill (the median of the local dark background) and the paste area, and therefore change exactly the same number of RGB pixels; collisions, divider clearance and background agreement are verified before any score is computed. Displacements range from 19 to 51 pixels.

Each variant has a target set $\T_i$ of compartments that require inspection: $\T_i=\{a_i,b_i\}$ for a fault, $\T_i=\{a_i\}$ for a vacancy, and $\T_i=\emptyset$ for a source or a sham, where every queued compartment is a false mark. Since the vacancy-only edit retains the empty half of the fault, comparing the two isolates the effect of the additional pin.

Every variant is scored through two interfaces with identical detector weights. The \emph{oracle} interface moves the pin directly in the released label map, while the \emph{extracted} interface re-segments every RGB variant, including the source, with an independent U-Net trained only on normal training photographs (97.3\% development pin IoU). Since a deployed system never observes ground-truth labels, we report the extracted interface unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Calibrated inspection queue}
\label{sec:queue}
We model the two decisions of an inspector, whether to open a tray and which compartments to check, with two cutoffs that are fixed before testing (\cref{alg:queue}): a scene cutoff $t_s\in[0,1]$ on the highest pixel score of a map, and a compartment cutoff $t_c\in[0,1]$ on the highest score inside a compartment. Writing $p$ for a pixel of the $256{\times}256$ grid, a tray raises an alarm when its highest pixel score exceeds the scene cutoff,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{alarm}_i=\mathbb{1}\bigl[\max_p S_i(p)>t_s\bigr],
 \label{eq:alarm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ equals 1 if its argument holds and 0 otherwise. Let $M_{(1)}\le\dots\le M_{(69)}$ be the sorted maxima $\max_p S_v(p)$ of 69 unedited validation photographs $v$. We set
\begin{equation}
 t_s=M_{(63)},\qquad 63=\lceil 0.9\,(69+1)\rceil,
 \label{eq:ts}
\end{equation}
a strict split-conformal cutoff~\cite{bates2023}: a new normal tray, exchangeable with the validation trays, exceeds $t_s$ with probability at most $1-63/70=10\%$. Each compartment then receives its maximum score $g_i(e)=\max S_i|_e$, and the inspection queue at compartment cutoff $t_c$ is
\begin{equation}
 \Q_i=\begin{cases}\{e\in\E:\ g_i(e)\ge t_c\} & \text{if }\mathrm{alarm}_i=1,\\ \emptyset & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
 \label{eq:queue}
\end{equation}
so that the targets of a tray without an alarm count as missed. Over $n$ faults, the target recall $R$ and the inspection cost $C$ are
\begin{equation}
 R=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n|\Q_i\cap\T_i|,\qquad
 C=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n|\Q_i\setminus\T_i|,
 \label{eq:rc}
\end{equation}
\ie, the fraction of target compartments that are queued and the mean number of untouched compartments queued per fault, which ranges from 0 to 13. For example, a fault whose queue contains both targets and three other compartments contributes $2/2$ to recall and $3$ to cost.

The compartment cutoff is fitted on 69 matched \emph{development} faults $\mathcal{D}$, constructed in the same way from validation photographs, and never on test data. Let $G=\{g_j(e): j\in\mathcal{D},\,e\in\T_j\}$ be the multiset of the $2\times69=138$ development target scores, with $g_j(e)=0$ for a fault without an alarm, and let $G^{(k)}$ denote its $k$-th largest element. The \emph{95\% cutoff} is the highest threshold that attains 95\% target recall on these faults,
\begin{equation}
 t_c^{95}=G^{(\lceil 0.95\,|G|\rceil)}=G^{(132)}.
 \label{eq:tc}
\end{equation} A \emph{fixed-cost cutoff} instead admits at most 0.5 untouched compartments per development fault and is the 35th largest untouched score, since $\lfloor0.5\cdot69\rfloor+1=35$; for this cutoff the comparison in \cref{eq:queue} is strict ($g_i(e)>t_c$). Both cutoffs are fitted separately for each map and interface, and on test data we report the achieved $R$ and $C$ together with alarm rates on all four variants.

We also report how often both targets are the two highest-scoring compartments (\emph{top 2}), the mean rank of the second target, a top-$k$ \emph{list} that queues the $k$ highest compartments with $k$ fitted like $t_c$, and, as a counterpart to sPRO~\cite{bergmann2022}, the \emph{region area}: target recall integrated up to a false-compartment rate of 0.05 over all compartments, with untouched, source and sham compartments as negatives. Region area is computed on test data without the scene alarm, so it measures ranking rather than the cost at an operating point.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Calibrated inspection queue}
\label{alg:queue}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require map model $f$; cells $\E$; normal validation photos $\mathcal{V}$; development faults $\mathcal{D}$ with targets $\T_j$
\State $t_s\gets$ 63rd smallest of $\{\max_p f(m_v)(p)\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}$ \Comment{$|\mathcal{V}|=69$}
\State $G\gets\{g_j(e): j\in\mathcal{D},\ e\in\T_j\}$, with $g_j(e)=0$ if $j$ has no alarm
\State $t_c\gets$ the $\lceil 0.95\,|G|\rceil$-th largest value of $G$ \Comment{95\% cutoff}
\For{each test photo $i$}
  \State $S_i\gets f(m_i)$;\ \textbf{if} $\max_p S_i(p)\le t_s$ \textbf{then} $\Q_i\gets\emptyset$
  \State \textbf{else} $\Q_i\gets\{e\in\E: \max S_i|_e\ge t_c\}$
\EndFor
\State \Return $R$ and $C$ from \cref{eq:rc}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Footprint supervision}
\label{sec:targets}
SALAD trains its composition branch on synthetic edits of normal part maps. For an edited map, a discriminative U-Net predicts a logit $z_p\in\mathbb{R}$ at every pixel $p$ of the pixel set $P$, so that $\sigma(z_p)=1/(1+e^{-z_p})$ is the predicted anomaly probability, and a binary target mask $y\in\{0,1\}^{P}$ states which pixels should be called anomalous. With the sigmoid focal loss $\mathrm{FL}(z,y)=-\alpha_y(1-q)^{\gamma}\log q$, where $q=\sigma(z)$ if $y=1$ and $q=1-\sigma(z)$ otherwise, $\alpha_1=\alpha=0.25$, $\alpha_0=1-\alpha$ and $\gamma=2$~\cite{lin2017focal}, the branch minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}=5\cdot\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_p \mathrm{FL}(z_p,y_p)+\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_p\bigl|\sigma(z_p)-y_p\bigr|,
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where the first term emphasizes hard pixels, the second penalizes the absolute probability error, and the weight 5 is that of the released implementation. Since $y$ is the only supervision of the map, it determines which pixels the branch learns to score high. When a part of class $k$ is inserted, producing the edited map $m'$, the released implementation marks every pixel of that class~\cite{fucka2025},
\begin{equation}
 y^{\text{cw}}(p)=\mathbb{1}[m'(p)=k]\qquad\text{(class-wide)},
\end{equation}
reflecting the view that an extra screw in a bag may be any of the screws. In a tray, however, only one compartment is over-full, which motivates marking only the footprint $F$ of the pasted donor,
\begin{equation}
 y^{\text{fp}}(p)=\mathbb{1}[p\in F]\qquad\text{(footprint)}.
\end{equation}
For relocations, both policies additionally mark the vacated footprint, shams always receive $y\equiv0$, and same-image relabelling targets are left unchanged, so the two policies differ only in the insertion part of the mask. In seed~1, a relocation marks on average 3,823 pixels under the class-wide policy and 461 under the footprint policy. The rule is used only to construct training edits; at test time the model observes a part map alone. Footprint targets need no extra information, because the generator knows $F$ for every edit. For an edited map $m'$ and a pixel $p$, let $q(m',p)$ be the probability, under the training distribution of edits, that the target at $p$ is 1.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:optimal}
Let the pixel loss $\ell(s,y)$ be non-increasing in the score $s$ for $y=1$ and non-decreasing for $y=0$, as are cross-entropy, the focal loss, the L1 loss on $\sigma$ and their sum in \cref{eq:loss}. Then the minimizer of the conditional risk $r(s;q)=q\,\ell(s,1)+(1-q)\,\ell(s,0)$ can be chosen non-decreasing in $q$. Hence (i), which predicts spread: under class-wide targets every pixel of class $k$ has $q$ at least the posterior probability that $m'$ contains an insertion of class $k$, so when an insertion is evident an optimal head raises all of them; and (ii), which predicts where footprint targets cannot help: under footprint targets, if the inserted instance is one of $m$ instances that the map cannot tell apart, its pixels have $q$ at most $1/m$ plus the probability of the other edits.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\partial_q\partial_s r=\partial_s\ell(s,1)-\partial_s\ell(s,0)\le0$, so $r$ has decreasing differences in $(s,q)$ and Topkis' theorem~\cite{topkis1978} gives a minimizer non-decreasing in $q$. For (i), every class-$k$ pixel of a map with a class-$k$ insertion has $y^{\text{cw}}=1$; for (ii), the posterior probability that a given one of $m$ indistinguishable instances was pasted is at most $1/m$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
The primary comparison is the paired difference in cost between the footprint and class-wide policies on extracted maps,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta C=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(|\Q_i^{\text{fp}}\setminus\T_i|-|\Q_i^{\text{cw}}\setminus\T_i|\Bigr),
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
where $\Q_i^{\text{fp}}$ and $\Q_i^{\text{cw}}$ are the queues of the same fault under each policy at its own 95\% cutoff; negative values favour the footprint policy. Before testing, we specified that a useful reduction must remove at least one untouched compartment with a 95\% interval excluding zero, while keeping the achieved recall at or above 90\% and within 5 points of the control, the fault and vacancy alarm rates at or above 95\% and within 3 points, and the increase in sham alarms below 5 points. Attributing the effect to the target additionally requires that the class-wide control meet a \emph{competence criterion} on development data: alarm rates and compartment AUROC of at least 90\%. Intervals are computed from 4,000 bootstrap resamples of the original photographs, in which all variants, policies and interfaces of a photograph are resampled together, and are conditional on the fitted models and cutoffs. Two seeds and two fixed halves of the test photographs (63 and 75) assess stability. On the real logical defects of MVTec LOCO we report AU-sPRO up to a 5\% false-positive rate~\cite{bergmann2022}, re-implemented at map resolution (within 0.7 points of the official code on four heads), and, for pushpins, $R$ and $C$ at the development-fixed operating point, where a compartment is a target when its annotation covers at least 20 pixels. These use parameters refit on development relocations pooled with vacancies, since real trays have one or several faulty compartments.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Each fine-tune starts from the released SALAD composition weights with the reconstruction autoencoder frozen. A batch contains two clean maps, one SALAD-style synthetic edit and one rule-aware edit (a relocation, sham or vacancy) built from the official training photographs. We train for 800 Adam steps and keep the final checkpoint, using two seeds. Within a seed, the two policies receive identical inputs, edits, optimization steps, loss weights and pixel weights, and differ only in $y$. Two further recipes, used in \cref{sec:scratch,sec:native}, train both arms in one process on the same prefetched stream of SALAD edits (no rule-aware edits): a \emph{from-scratch} recipe that trains the whole composition branch from random initialization with SALAD's published 70{,}000 steps (pushpins, two seeds), and a 1.6k-step \emph{continuation} of the released head in five categories. Further details are given in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Diagnosis of the released model}
\label{sec:released}
The released composition branch raises an alarm on all 138 faults and all 138 vacancies under both interfaces, and scores every fault above its matched sham (138/138; exact 95\% interval 97.4--100\%). On oracle maps, the AUROC of the affected compartments rises from 48.6\% on the source, which is chance level, to 98.2\% after the fault. The ranking is also accurate: on extracted maps, both targets are among the two highest-scoring compartments for 77.5\% of faults and among the three highest for 94.2\%, and the second target appears at rank 2.43 on average. Scoring compartments by their 99th percentile instead of their maximum raises the top-2 rate to 87.0\% ($+9.4$ percentage points, pp, 95\% interval [4.3, 14.5]) with the same alarm and weights.

Good ranking does not, however, translate into a short queue. At the 95\% development cutoff, the released map recovers 95.7\% of the target compartments but queues 11.07 of the 13 untouched compartments per fault (\cref{tab:inspection}), so that the inspector examines nearly the entire tray. The 99th-percentile readout, although better at rank two, is no better here (11.41 at 96.0\%), and the 98\% cutoff raises the cost to 12.56. Conversely, when the cost is held to 0.30 compartments by the fixed-cost cutoff, the released map recovers only 81.9\% of the targets (\cref{fig:maps}d). A readout that is preferable for a fixed list length can therefore be inferior at a fixed recall.

The reason is that the fault alters the released map across almost the entire tray (\cref{fig:collateral}). On oracle maps, 90.3\% of untouched compartments contain a pixel whose score changes by at least 0.5 (only 5.4\% of their pixels do, so the peaks are sharp). The vacancy-only edit, which leaves the same empty compartment, affects only 2.9\% of the untouched compartments and the sham 0.7\%. The fault-minus-vacancy difference is 87.4 points [82.4, 91.9] (86.6 [81.4, 91.2] after extraction): the additional pin, not the vacancy, causes the widespread response, and an insertion probe shows that the response follows the inserted class (\cref{fig:collateral}b).

\subsection{Effect of footprint supervision}
\label{sec:footprint}
Fine-tuning with footprint targets reduces the cost at 95\% recall from 11.07 untouched compartments to 0.20 and 0.45 in the two seeds, at an achieved recall of 95.3\% and 96.0\% (\cref{tab:inspection}). The paired reductions relative to the released model are 10.88 [10.12, 11.56] and 10.62 [9.86, 11.31] compartments, and relative to the class-wide fine-tunes, the pre-specified control, 12.80 [12.72, 12.87] and 12.55 [12.45, 12.65] (supplement, paired-effects figure). Every fault and vacancy still raises an alarm, and all recall differences have intervals that include zero and remain above the 5-point floor. The reduction holds on oracle maps and on both fixed halves of the photographs (supplement). \Cref{fig:maps} illustrates a typical case in which the released map queues all 13 untouched compartments and the footprint map queues none.

The advantage holds at the other operating points. At the fixed-cost cutoff, the footprint heads recover 100.0\% and 98.2\% of targets at achieved costs of 0.38 and 0.57, compared with 81.9\% at 0.30 for the released model. The region area increases from 81.6\% to 96.3\% and 89.6\%; this ranking-based summary agrees in direction but does not reveal how much shorter the calibrated queue becomes.

Two secondary outcomes depend on the seed: sham alarms rise from 7 to 9 and 20 (within the margin set against the control), and the top-2 rate rises to 87.7\% for seed~1 but falls to 74.6\% for seed~2.

The class-wide fine-tunes, in contrast, retain every alarm but assign high scores to the entire tray: all 13 untouched compartments are queued and the top-2 rate is zero. With a development compartment AUROC of about 48\%, they fail the competence criterion of \cref{sec:protocol}.

\subsection{Continuation of the original recipe}
\label{sec:upstream}
To separate the effect of the target from that of the rule-aware edits, we also continue training with SALAD's original synthetic edit families only, replacing the rule-aware slot with a clean map. The class-wide head of seed~2 passes the competence criterion (97.3\% development AUROC) and reproduces the behaviour of the released model, with 88.8\% of untouched compartments changing by at least 0.5. Footprint targets reduce this fraction to 1.3\% and the cost from 11.50 to 7.07, a paired reduction of 4.43 [3.72, 5.14] at 96.0\% and 95.3\% recall. Seed~1 shows a similar reduction (4.28), but its control does not reach the AUROC criterion. Footprint targets therefore shorten the queue even without rule-aware training, while the rule-aware edits yield the lowest costs.

The gain in localization does not depend on the recipe (supplement, per-category table). In 1.6k-step continuations footprint targets raise pushpin AU-sPRO from \ppCW\% to \ppFP\% (\ppGain{} points), and across all six matched pushpin pairs of three recipes footprint targets reach \sixFP\% and class-wide targets \sixCW\%. Breakfast boxes improve from \bbCW\% to \bbFP\%, driven by missing cereals; juice bottles and splicing connectors, whose parts are single instances, show no consistent change. \subsection{Interchangeable parts}
\label{sec:ambiguous}
Screw bags, SALAD's motivating case, count interchangeable screws, nuts and washers, and MVTec LOCO annotates an extra nut over every nut in the bag, whereas an extra pushpin is annotated only in its compartment. \Cref{prop:optimal}(ii) predicts that footprint targets cannot help here, and they do not: AU-sPRO is \sbFP\% against \sbCW\% (\sbGain{} points), and on extra parts the footprint heads localize worst. Footprint targets still remove the same-class spread (\cref{fig:collateral}b); what differs is the task's definition of where the fault is.

\subsection{What valid shams reveal}
\label{sec:shams}
Valid shams also expose maps that respond to the edit rather than the rule. EfficientAD~\cite{batzner2024} and PatchCore~\cite{roth2022}, which read photographs, localize the targets of faulty trays better than the composition branch (fault-only region area \eadRegFault\% and \pcRegFault\% against \relRegFault\%), but they alarm on \eadSham{} and \pcSham{} of 138 shams, and counting sham and source compartments as negatives reverses the ranking (\eadRegAll\% and \pcRegAll\% against \relRegAll\%). SALAD's full system reaches 100.0\% logical AUROC on native pushpins, yet at a 10\% normal cutoff it alarms on 130 shams and 75 sources; its composition branch alarms on 7 shams. The supplement adds a second rule (a juice-bottle icon that must match the liquid), where the global appearance branch rather than the composition branch separates faults from shams, and a graph counterpart, in which a masked link predictor recovers switched links through node degree.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
We asked how much inspection effort a detector that catches every fault still imposes, and what in its training determines that effort. The released composition branch detects every fault, yet at high recall it directs the inspector to 11 of 13 untouched compartments. The cost is set by the training target: marking every part of the inserted class teaches the network that one extra pin makes the whole tray anomalous, and marking only the pasted part reduces the cost to below half a compartment. Trained from scratch this way, the detector finds more defective compartments on real pushpin trays while opening fewer intact ones than any policy on the released map. Where parts are interchangeable and annotated as a set, as in screw bags, the class-wide target remains the better match, so the extent of a synthetic label belongs to the task specification.

Our edits are composites of one tray; ranking, sham specificity and the from-scratch class-wide control depend on the seed; footprint heads raise more sham alarms; and short continuations do not beat the released list on cost. Physical rearrangements and other detectors trained on synthetic insertions are the next tests.
