\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked why a logical anomaly detector that flags every faulty tray can still send an inspector to almost every compartment. For SALAD the answer is one design choice. Labelling a synthetic insertion over its whole class teaches the map to light up every part of that class, as \cref{prop:optimal} predicts and as identical-input retraining confirms. Labelling only the footprint that was pasted removes this spread and more than doubles localization of real pushpin defects. Trained from scratch with SALAD's full recipe, the footprint detector finds more of the faulty compartments on real defects while opening fewer intact ones than any policy on the released map. It improves breakfast boxes as well and leaves juice bottles and splicing connectors unchanged. The fix costs nothing, because every synthetic edit already knows its footprint.

The screw-bag result marks the boundary of this advice. Where the task treats instances as interchangeable and annotates all of them, the class-wide label is the better match. The general lesson is that the spatial extent of a synthetic label is part of the task specification. It should follow the task's own definition of where a fault is, which a benchmark's annotation and saturation rules often state explicitly. Matched rearrangements make this choice testable, and their valid shams also distinguish maps that respond to a violation from maps that respond to an edit, as the appearance detectors and SALAD's full system show.

Our evidence has limits. The matched edits are composites of real photographs and cover one kind of fault; the re-segmented path still carries compositing traces that the oracle path removes. The full-recipe replication covers pushpins only, and its class-wide arm reproduces the released spread in one of the two seeds. Footprint labels lower pushpin image AUROC by about 1.5 points in continuation, with no consistent cost from scratch, and they raise valid-sham alarms at the development scene cutoff. We study one detector family, because the intervention requires localization learned from labelled insertions, and our AU-sPRO is a re-implementation of the official rules at map resolution. Promising directions include labels whose extent is chosen per class from the annotation semantics, set-valued targets that express ``one of these instances'' for interchangeable parts, the same intervention in other detectors trained on synthetic insertions, and physical rearrangements that remove compositing altogether.
